*Held like FINDINGS. Drafted 22/10/2026 from the results computed under the sealed declaration `bc052984…` (for the sponsor's own check: `FINDINGS.md` as rendered at SHA-256 `9a3557f4…`, `evidence/results.json` `f5db2ec0…`). The sponsor adopts from this sheet; nothing travels from FINDINGS directly. No project is named and no internal source is named. Each figure carries the number and its unit, the clock it rests on, the copy range, the kill condition or interval it must travel with, and the one caveat without which it misleads.*

## What was measured, in four sentences

A project is a chain of rows in NESO's TEC Register, followed across the register's three re-keyings by a declared matching rule. A chain has "left the register" when it is last printed at least 95 days before the last copy read (20 October 2026) and its last printed status was not Built; a chain that reappears under a matched key is not an exit, and a project that changes technology under the same chain is a reclassification, never an exit. The trigger date is 1 April three financial years before the financial year of the connection date, as CUSC Section 15 (Part Two, 2.2(a)) states it; clock A reads the connection date from the chain's last printed row, clock B from its first, clock C from each month's row. Rates are exits per 100 chain-years at risk in each band, with 95 % Poisson intervals, so a crowded band is not mistaken for bunching.

## Which figures rest on what

Figures 1 to 7 rest on the **thin storage series**: 96 storage exits before the connections-reform break, which is under the declaration's floor of 200 (kill condition (b) fired), so every storage rate below is a described measurement with a wide interval and is not called a pattern. Figures 8 to 10 rest on **all technologies** or on the register as a whole.

## The ten figures

1. **96 storage projects left the TEC Register between January 2014 and the reform break**, out of 2,644 project chains of every technology, of which 494 left. Clock: none (a count). Copies: exit copies 31 January 2014 to 28 November 2025, series read to 20 October 2026. Travels with: kill condition (b), fewer than 200 storage exits. Caveat: "left the register" is absence from the public register for 95 days or more, not a termination; the register alone cannot say whether a project was removed, withdrew, or was novated.

2. **In the twelve months before the trigger date, storage projects left at 2.17 per 100 chain-years (interval 0.79 to 4.73), against 1.61 (1.13 to 2.21) more than two years before it.** Clock A. Copies: as figure 1. Travels with: both intervals, which overlap; 6 exits in the year-before band. Caveat: this is the absence of a difference the series could have seen, not proof of equality; a bunching of a few exits a year would be invisible at this size.

3. **The same holds under the other two clocks: 2.87 (1.15 to 5.91) against 2.22 (1.56 to 3.05) when the trigger is read from the first printed connection date, and 2.01 (0.73 to 4.38) against 2.12 (1.49 to 2.92) when exposure follows the date as it stood each month.** Clocks B and C. Copies: as figure 1. Travels with: the intervals. Caveat: for chains already on the register in January 2014, "first printed" means the first copy held, not the date first contracted.

4. **More than twelve months after the trigger read from the last printed connection date, storage projects left at 12.20 per 100 chain-years (8.40 to 17.13), about seven times the far-before rate.** Clock A only. Copies: as figure 1. Travels with: the flatness test, which fails under clock A and passes under clocks B (2.89 in that band) and C (3.78). Caveat: the rise is a property of the clock, not of the bill: a project that leaves with a stale or passed connection date sits far "after" its trigger by construction, so this reads as projects leaving late in their own printed programme.

5. **Against Ofgem's 72 storage projects that "exited or changed classification" in 2023 to 2025, this series counts 65 storage exits with exit copies from January 2023 to November 2025 (8, 25 and 32 by year) plus 16 storage chains reclassified away from storage between January snapshots.** Clock: none. Copies: snapshots 6 January 2023, 5 January 2024, 3 January 2025 and 28 November 2025. Travels with: the declaration's reconciliation test, which holds (counts meet within a factor of two). Caveat: the two populations differ; Ofgem's is NESO's whole connections queue on unpublished snapshot dates, this is the TEC Register, so 72 and 65 plus 16 are neighbours, not the same count.

6. **Of storage chains present at a January snapshot, 0.0 %, 1.4 % and 2.2 % had left the register by the next snapshot, against Ofgem's 6.7 % a year.** Clock: none. Copies: as figure 5 (323, 804 and 1,177 storage chains present at the three snapshots). Travels with: figure 5's caveat on the population. Caveat: the last pair spans eleven months, and a snapshot pair that straddles a re-keying copy would show every project leaving unless chains are stitched; the August 2022 re-issue sits just before Ofgem's first window.

7. **By stage, storage chains at Scoping or Awaiting Consents left at 3.42 per 100 chain-years over the whole old regime, Consents Approved at 0.39, Under Construction at 2.02, beside Ofgem's 7.4 %, 3 % and 0 % a year.** Clock A exposure, stage at last sighting. Copies: as figure 1. Travels with: kill condition (b). Caveat: the direction agrees (earlier stages leave more) but the Under Construction rate rests on two exits, and a rate per 100 chain-years matches a percentage a year only when exposure is counted the same way.

8. **All technologies together: 4.43 per 100 chain-years more than two years before the trigger, 11.39 at 12 to 24 months before, 11.77 in the year before, 13.22 in the year after and 26.28 beyond that (485 exits before the reform break).** Clock A. Copies: as figure 1. Travels with: the intervals (3.79 to 5.15 for the first band, 22.02 to 31.13 for the last). Caveat: the step up two years before the trigger comes from the control, mostly wind and gas chains of 2014 to 2021, and storage alone does not show it; 2014's 94 exits are the register settling into a stable format, not a wave, and two of the 494 chains are print artefacts of a single December 2020 copy (footer text read as rows; neither is storage and neither carries a connection date).

10. **Of the exits whose customer could be linked to a Companies House company (24.3 % of all exits, about half of storage exits), 37.5 % had a live charge registered against the company on the exit date; the companies are almost all still active.** Clock: none. Copies: as figure 1, Companies House read 22 October 2026. Travels with: the unlinked share (75.7 %). Caveat: linkage used company names resolved on the register's current identifiers, so customers that left before 2021 are mostly unlinked, and a registered charge is security over the company, not evidence of a connection security posted with NESO.

## What is not on this sheet, and why

The planning (Renewable Energy Planning Database) and Capacity Market joins matched under a fifth of storage exits, below the declared 70 % floor, so no planning or Capacity Market class is quotable; the matched subsets are in FINDINGS for the sponsor only. No cause of exit, no securities posted, no liability accrued and no zonal tariff is claimed: NESO publishes no site-to-zone list, so the liability profile was not computed.

## What the appendix join key offers Ofgem

Every chain that left the register carries, in the held appendix, a join key made of the fields Ofgem's own per-project table holds: project name, customer name, connection site, Project ID and Project Number where the register printed them, stage MW, and the dates of first and last sighting, with the exit month and the band under each clock beside it. Ofgem's Initial Impact Assessment records that NESO gave Ofgem a per-project list of every Gate 2 battery project with its current and maximum securities and liabilities, and the 2023 to 2025 queues. Applied on Ofgem's side, the key puts each exit's month and clock position beside the securities actually posted, which is the one join that turns "trigger date" into "securities rising" in Ofgem's own words, and it does so without this project ever seeing Ofgem's table. The chain's re-keying record travels with the key, so a project Ofgem cannot match can be traced to a re-keying or a stitch rather than guessed at.
